## The problem

The body is a few homogeneous regions: a plate, the rock under it, perhaps a
slab. They meet on interfaces, faults cut them, and the whole is cut from the
Earth by the walls of a box. A fault slips at $t=0$ and stays slipped. Wanted
are the displacement $\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)$ and the stress
$\boldsymbol\sigma(\mathbf x,t)$ everywhere, at every later time.

Inertia is dropped. The times of interest are years, and a wave crosses the
model in minutes, so at each instant the stress is in equilibrium,

$$
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma(\mathbf x,t)=\mathbf 0,
\qquad
\boldsymbol\epsilon=\tfrac12\big(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}\big),
$$

with small strains. Across an interface the displacement and the traction
$\boldsymbol\sigma\,\mathbf n$ are continuous. Across a fault with normal
$\mathbf n$ the traction is continuous and the displacement jumps by the
slip,

$$
\mathbf b=\mathbf u(+\mathbf n)-\mathbf u(-\mathbf n).
$$

On an outer face either the traction or the displacement is given. Gravity
is taken up [below](#gravity); it turns out to be one more condition of this
kind. None of these statements has time in it. Time enters through the
material, and only there.

## The material

Each region is isotropic and linear, and it remembers. Its stress is the sum
of what every past increment of strain still contributes,

$$
\boldsymbol\sigma(t)=\int_{0^-}^{t}\Big[\,2\,\mu(t-t')\,
\operatorname{dev}\dot{\boldsymbol\epsilon}(t')
+K\,\operatorname{tr}\dot{\boldsymbol\epsilon}(t')\,\mathbf I\,\Big]\,dt',
$$

where $\mu(t)$ is the *relaxation modulus* in shear: the shear stress left at
time $t$ after a unit step of shear strain, over twice that strain. The bulk
modulus $K$ is a constant. Rock flows in shear and does not flow in
compression, so only the deviatoric part relaxes. One consequence is carried
through everything below: Poisson's ratio is not a constant of the material.
It rises with time, and reaches $1/2$ in a region that has relaxed
completely.

Two materials are implemented.

**Maxwell.** A spring $\mu$ and a dashpot $\eta$ in series: the strain rates
add,
$\operatorname{dev}\dot{\boldsymbol\epsilon}
=\operatorname{dev}\dot{\boldsymbol\sigma}/2\mu
+\operatorname{dev}\boldsymbol\sigma/2\eta$, and

$$
\mu(t)=\mu\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad\tau=\frac{\eta}{\mu}.
$$

It is a fluid. Held at a fixed strain it forgets its stress; held at a fixed
stress it creeps for ever.

**Burgers.** A Maxwell element in series with a Kelvin element, a spring
$\mu_K$ beside a dashpot $\eta_K$. It relaxes twice: by a transient that is
over in a time of order $\tau_K=\eta_K/\mu_K$, then steadily. It has [a
section of its own](#a-burgers-material) below.

A rheology is stated in relaxation *times* and *ratios* of moduli, never in
viscosities: a viscosity only ever appears as $\eta/\mu$. So time has no unit
of its own here. Everything on this site is in Maxwell times of the
substrate, and $\tau$ is 10.6 years for $\eta=10^{19}$&nbsp;Pa&nbsp;s and
$\mu=30$&nbsp;GPa.

## Correspondence

Take the Laplace transform in time,
$\hat f(s)=\int_0^\infty f(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt$. A convolution becomes a product,
so the law above becomes

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\sigma}=2\,\tilde\mu(s)\,\operatorname{dev}\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon}
+K\,\operatorname{tr}(\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon})\,\mathbf I,
\qquad
\tilde\mu(s)=s\,\hat\mu(s).
$$

That is Hooke's law, with an *operational* shear modulus that depends on $s$:

| material | $\tilde\mu(s)$ | $s\to\infty$ | $s\to0$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maxwell | $\mu\,\dfrac{s}{s+1/\tau}$ | $\mu$ | $0$ |
| Burgers | $\dfrac1{\tilde\mu}=\dfrac1\mu+\dfrac1{\mu\,\tau_M\,s}+\dfrac1{\mu_K\,(1+\tau_K s)}$ | $\mu$ | $0$ |

Equilibrium, the definition of strain and the conditions on interfaces and
faults are linear and have no time in them, so they transform unchanged. The
transformed problem is therefore an *elastic* problem on the same geometry,
each region with the moduli

$$
\tilde\mu(s),\qquad\tilde\lambda(s)=K-\tfrac23\,\tilde\mu(s).
$$

This is the correspondence principle (Lee 1955). It asks two things of the
problem. The *kind* of condition on every boundary must not change with time:
a face that is given a displacement stays one. And the data must not hide a
modulus: a traction written as a stiffness times a strain is not data.

**Step data.** If everything prescribed is switched on at $t=0$ and held, a
datum $d_0H(t)$ has the transform $d_0/s$, and

$$
\tilde{\mathbf u}(\mathbf x,s)\equiv s\,\hat{\mathbf u}(\mathbf x,s)
$$

is *exactly the elastic solution* with the moduli of $s$ and the data $d_0$.
Nothing but the material changes from one $s$ to the next. Large $s$ is the
instant of the earthquake and small $s$ is long after it,

$$
\mathbf u(0^+)=\lim_{s\to\infty}\tilde{\mathbf u}(s),\qquad
\mathbf u(\infty)=\lim_{s\to0}\tilde{\mathbf u}(s),
$$

the second being the statement that a completely relaxed body is an elastic
one with relaxed moduli (Fukahata and Matsu'ura 2006). A step is all that is
needed: the problem is linear, so any history of slip is a sum of steps, and
its response is the step response convolved with the slip rate.

## One elastic problem per state

At one value of $s$ the problem is elastic, and it is solved as
[mbem](https://brendanjmeade.github.io/mbem/concepts/) solves any elastic
problem: on the boundaries. Inside a region $R$ the displacement is fixed by
what happens on its surface and on the faults within it,

$$
\mathbf u(\mathbf x)=\int_{\partial R}\Big[\mathbf G^\varepsilon(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\,
\mathbf t(\mathbf y)-\mathbf T^\varepsilon(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\,\mathbf u(\mathbf y)\Big]dS_y
+\int_{F}\mathbf T^\varepsilon(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\,\mathbf b(\mathbf y)\,dS_y .
$$

$\mathbf G^\varepsilon$ is the displacement of a point force and
$\mathbf T^\varepsilon$ the traction that goes with it, formed with the
normal out of $R$ on its boundary and with the fault's own normal on $F$.
Taking $\mathbf x$ to the boundary gives, for each region, one equation
between the displacement and the traction on its surface. Two regions that
share an interface share its displacement and its traction; on an outer face
one of the two is given. In the models here the unknowns are constant on
each triangle and the equations are collocated at the centroids.

The superscript is what makes this method the one it is. The force is not a
point but a blob of width $\varepsilon$, so the kernels are finite
everywhere, on the element itself included, and each triangle's integral is
done in closed form:

$$
\mathbf G^\varepsilon=\frac1\mu\Big[\boldsymbol\Gamma_A+g\,\boldsymbol\Gamma_B\Big],\qquad
\Gamma_{A,ij}=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{4\pi R_\varepsilon}
+\frac{\varepsilon^2\delta_{ij}}{8\pi R_\varepsilon^3},\qquad
\Gamma_{B,ij}=\frac1{16\pi}\Big(\frac{d_id_j}{R_\varepsilon^3}
-\frac{\delta_{ij}}{R_\varepsilon}\Big),
$$

with $\mathbf d=\mathbf x-\mathbf y$,
$R_\varepsilon=\sqrt{\lvert\mathbf d\rvert^2+\varepsilon^2}$ and

$$
g=\frac1{1-\nu}.
$$

Mollification is geometry. It commutes with the transform: the mollified
kernel at the moduli of $s$ is the transform of the response to the same
blob.

## What changes between states

Written that way, the kernel shows where the material is. Poisson's ratio
enters only through $g$, and the shear modulus only as a factor. The same is
true of every operator the method uses:

| operator | form |
|---|---|
| traction to displacement, $\mathbf G^\varepsilon$ | $\mu^{-1}\,(U_A+g\,U_B)$ |
| slip or boundary displacement to displacement, $\mathbf T^\varepsilon$ | $T_A+g\,T_B$ |
| slip to elastic stress | $\mu\,(S_A+g\,S_B)$ |

Every $A$ and $B$ depends on the mesh and on $\varepsilon$ and on nothing
else. A region's whole dependence on $s$ is three scalars,
$\tilde\mu$, $1/\tilde\mu$ and

$$
g(s)=\frac{2\,\big(3K+\tilde\mu(s)\big)}{3K+4\,\tilde\mu(s)},
$$

which runs from its elastic value up to $2$ as the region relaxes. So a
model is assembled once, and a state is that assembly recombined.

Two things in that table are not obvious.

**Nothing is singular as a region becomes a fluid.** The slip kernel has a
term in $\lambda$, and $\lambda/\mu$ grows without bound as $\nu\to1/2$. But
it multiplies the divergence of $\boldsymbol\Gamma_A+g\,\boldsymbol\Gamma_B$,
which carries the factor $g-2=-(1-2\nu)/(1-\nu)$, and the product is
$-2\,(g-1)$: finite, mollified terms included. $\mathbf T^\varepsilon$ has no
$\mu$ in it at all.

**The stress has this form only once the eigenstress is removed.** A
mollified fault does not jump; it smears its slip over the width
$\varepsilon$. The smeared slip is an eigenstrain $\boldsymbol\epsilon^*$,
fixed in space, and the elastic stress is

$$
\boldsymbol\sigma=\mathbf C:\big(\nabla\mathbf u-\boldsymbol\epsilon^*\big).
$$

The raw $\mathbf C:\nabla\mathbf u$ differs from it by
$2\mu\,\boldsymbol\epsilon^*+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol\epsilon^*)\,\mathbf I$,
and $\lambda/\mu=2\,(g-1)/(2-g)$ is not linear in $g$ and is unbounded at
$g=2$. Removing the eigenstress is what makes a stress an elastic stress in
the first place; it is also what lets a region relax.

## One mollification width

With a single blob the kernel is one exact elastic field, and two
identities hold on any closed surface $\partial V$. They hold on the faceted
mesh itself, to rounding, because each triangle is integrated exactly.

A rigid translation costs nothing. The traction of the blob's field
integrates to minus the force enclosed,

$$
\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf T^\varepsilon(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)\,dS_y
=-\,\mathbf I\int_V\phi_\varepsilon(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)\,dV_y ,
$$

and there is no material in that. The diagonal of the equations is this sum,
so it is the same at every $s$.

A uniform pressure moves nothing in an incompressible body. At $g=2$,
$\mu\,\mathbf G^\varepsilon$ is the regularised Stokeslet of Cortez (2001),
which is divergence-free, so

$$
\oint_{\partial V}\big(\boldsymbol\Gamma_A+2\,\boldsymbol\Gamma_B\big)\,\mathbf n\,dS_y=\mathbf 0 .
$$

This null mode is what leaves the small compressibility, alone, to set the
pressure of a region that has nearly relaxed.

If each element is given its own width, the sum is no longer one field and
both identities fail by the amount the width varies over the surface. The
failure of the second is amplified by $K/\tilde\mu(s)$, which is what a
relaxing region makes large. Measured on a sphere in a relaxing host, the
hydrostatic strain against its closed form:

| $\tilde\mu(s)/K$ | a width per element | one width |
|---|---|---|
| $4.6\times10^{-3}$ | $-0.2$&nbsp;% | $-0.1$&nbsp;% |
| $4.6\times10^{-4}$ | $-9.4$&nbsp;% | $-0.2$&nbsp;% |
| $4.6\times10^{-5}$ | $-54$&nbsp;% | $-0.2$&nbsp;% |
| $4.6\times10^{-6}$ | $-95$&nbsp;% | $-0.2$&nbsp;% |

Hence the one rule relaxation adds to the elastic method: **the closed
boundary of a region that relaxes carries one mollification width.** With it
the solve is unchanged down to $\nu=0.499998$.

## Poles

Each of the three scalars is a ratio of polynomials in $s$ whose poles are
real and not positive. For a Maxwell material, with $a=1/\tau$,

$$
\tilde\mu=\mu\,\frac{s}{s+a},\qquad
\frac1{\tilde\mu}=\frac1\mu\Big(1+\frac as\Big),\qquad
g(s)=2+(g_e-2)\,\frac{s}{s+1/\tau_2},\qquad
\tau_2=\tau\,\frac{3K+4\mu}{3K},
$$

and in time these are $\mu\,e^{-t/\tau}$, the steady creep
$(1+t/\tau)/\mu$ of a fluid, and $2+(g_e-2)\,e^{-t/\tau_2}$. A Burgers
material has two poles in $\tilde\mu$ and two more in $g$.

A solution built from such operators inherits their character: the response
of a relaxing body to a step is a constant plus decaying exponentials. Which
exponentials depends on the whole body and not only on its material. A plate
over a substrate goes on relaxing for many times the substrate's own Maxwell
time, and for longer the farther from the fault. Two cases differ in kind. With slip and displacements
prescribed, the body relaxes to a finite state. With a traction prescribed
on a fluid it never stops, the $1/s$ above, and its response has a term
linear in time: a crack held at a fixed stress drop slips for ever.

## Back to time

The elastic solve gives $\tilde{\mathbf u}(s)$ at whatever $s$ is asked for.
It is asked for at real, positive $s$, where the moduli are real and
positive and each state is an ordinary elastic problem for the same solver.
The points are laid out for the times wanted, $s=1/t$, log-spaced at three
per decade and carried half a decade past each end:

$$
s_k=\frac{10^{\,1/2}}{t_{\min}}\;10^{-k/3},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,
\quad\text{down to}\quad\frac{10^{-1/2}}{t_{\max}} .
$$

For times from 0.1 to 100 that is thirteen values, from $31.6$ to $0.0032$
per Maxwell time. The elastic state, $s=\infty$, makes fourteen.

Inverting a transform from its values on the real axis is ill-posed if
nothing is known of the answer. Here its form is known, so it is fitted, with
the relaxation times *fixed* at the reciprocals of the sample points,
$\tau_j=1/s_j$ (the collocation of Schapery 1962):

$$
\tilde{\mathbf u}(\mathbf x,s)=\mathbf a_0(\mathbf x)
+\sum_j\mathbf a_j(\mathbf x)\,\frac{s}{s+1/\tau_j}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)=\mathbf a_0+\sum_j\mathbf a_j\,e^{-t/\tau_j}.
$$

With the poles fixed the fit is linear, and with as many terms as states it
is a square system. Its matrix has no solution values in it,

$$
M_{k0}=1,\qquad M_{kj}=\frac{s_k}{s_k+1/\tau_j},
$$

the row of the elastic state being all ones. Write the fourteen solutions at
a point as a vector $\tilde{\mathbf u}$ and the functions of time as
$\mathbf e(t)=\big(1,\,e^{-t/\tau_1},\,\dots\big)$. Then
$\mathbf a=M^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf u}$ and

$$
\mathbf u(t)=\mathbf w(t)\cdot\tilde{\mathbf u},\qquad
\mathbf w(t)^{\mathsf T}=\mathbf e(t)^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}.
$$

A time is fourteen weights. They are the same for every unknown and every
point, and for anything linear in the solution: the displacement of the
surface, the slip of a fault that creeps, the stress at a point in the
mantle. Three properties follow from the matrix alone.

- At $t=0$ the weights select the elastic state and nothing else, because
  $\mathbf e(0)$ is that state's row of $M$. The earthquake itself is
  reproduced exactly.
- The weights always sum to one, because a solution that does not depend on
  $s$ is fitted by its constant.
- The relaxed state is $\mathbf a_0$, the first row of $M^{-1}$; the velocity
  is a second set of weights, $\dot{\mathbf e}(t)^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}$.

A body that creeps is fitted with one more term, $c/s$ for $c\,t$, in place
of the slowest exponential, which over the window is all but the same
function.

**How good it is.** Measured on transforms whose inverses are known exactly
(one pole, two, a repeated pole, a creeping response), the worst error over
three decades of time:

| points per decade | solves | $u$ | $t\,\dot u$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 9 | $4.4\times10^{-3}$ | $6.7\times10^{-2}$ |
| 3 | 13 | $9.5\times10^{-4}$ | $1.2\times10^{-2}$ |
| 4 | 17 | $4.2\times10^{-5}$ | $1.9\times10^{-3}$ |

Three is used: the inversion then sits well under the error of the mesh. A
velocity carries about 1&nbsp;%.

**Where it holds.** Nothing constrains the fit at times shorter than the
shortest $\tau_j$ or longer than the longest. A result is trusted inside the
window the points were laid out for and not outside it, which is why no
clock on this site runs past 100.

**Why not a contour.** A transform can be inverted with no assumption about
its form by sampling it on a contour in the complex plane. Each sample would
then be an elastic problem with complex moduli, and the sum cancels at
least as heavily as this one does. The contour is used here for what it is best at:
the tests invert closed-form transforms on one, to thirty digits, and hold
the real-axis fit to the result.

## The weights of a time

The weights are large and of both signs, and they cancel. Here they are for
the models on this site, at the time on the slider. A state much slower
than the time, one whose $1/s$ lies well beyond $t$, carries almost nothing.
The rest alternate in sign, and each can be tens of times their sum, which
is always one. How large they are depends on where the time falls among the

## A Burgers material

A Maxwell material has one way to relax. Rock has at least two: a fast
transient, and the steady flow behind it. The Burgers material is the
simplest that has both. A Maxwell element, spring $\mu$ and dashpot
$\eta_M$, is in series with a Kelvin element, a spring $\mu_K$ beside a
dashpot $\eta_K$. In series the deviatoric stress $\boldsymbol\sigma'$ is
shared and the strains add:

$$
\operatorname{dev}\boldsymbol\epsilon=\boldsymbol\epsilon_M+\boldsymbol\epsilon_K,\qquad
\dot{\boldsymbol\epsilon}_M=\frac{\dot{\boldsymbol\sigma}'}{2\mu}+\frac{\boldsymbol\sigma'}{2\eta_M},\qquad
\boldsymbol\sigma'=2\mu_K\,\boldsymbol\epsilon_K+2\eta_K\,\dot{\boldsymbol\epsilon}_K .
$$

Transform each, with nothing strained before $t=0$, and add the strains.
What multiplies the stress is the reciprocal of the operational modulus,

$$
\frac1{\tilde\mu(s)}=\frac1\mu+\frac1{\eta_M\,s}+\frac1{\mu_K+\eta_K\,s}:
$$

the compliances of the spring, the dashpot and the Kelvin element, added.
Under a step of stress that is the creep

$$
J(t)=\frac1\mu+\frac{t}{\eta_M}+\frac{1-e^{-t/\tau_K}}{\mu_K},
$$

an instant strain, a steady flow, and a transient that saturates.

**Two times and a ratio.** The material has three numbers beyond its
unrelaxed modulus. They are stated as $\tau_M=\eta_M/\mu$, the steady time;
$\tau_K=\eta_K/\mu_K$, the transient one; and $k=\mu_K/\mu$. Then

$$
\frac{\tilde\mu(s)}{\mu}
=\left[\,1+\frac1{\tau_M\,s}+\frac1{k\,(1+\tau_K\,s)}\right]^{-1}
=\frac{k\,\tau_M\,s\,(1+\tau_K\,s)}
{k\,\tau_M\tau_K\,s^2+\big(k\,\tau_M+k\,\tau_K+\tau_M\big)\,s+k}.
$$

Time is measured in $\tau_M$ throughout, so a Burgers model and a Maxwell
one on the same clock have the same steady viscosity.

**It is two Maxwell materials side by side.** The denominator is a quadratic
with two real, negative roots, $-1/\tau_1$ and $-1/\tau_2$, so the modulus
splits into two terms of the Maxwell kind,

$$
\tilde\mu(s)=\mu\left[A_1\,\frac{s}{s+1/\tau_1}+A_2\,\frac{s}{s+1/\tau_2}\right],
\qquad
\mu(t)=\mu\left[A_1\,e^{-t/\tau_1}+A_2\,e^{-t/\tau_2}\right],
\qquad A_1+A_2=1 .
$$

When the transient is much the faster, $\tau_K\ll\tau_M$,

$$
\tau_1\approx\tau_K\,\frac{k}{1+k},\quad A_1\approx\frac1{1+k};
\qquad
\tau_2\approx\tau_M\,\frac{1+k}{k},\quad A_2\approx\frac{k}{1+k}.
$$

The first branch is the transient: a fraction $1/(1+k)$ of the modulus, gone
in less than $\tau_K$. The second is *slower* than the Maxwell time, by
$(1+k)/k$, because what the transient leaves is a softer spring,
$\mu k/(1+k)$, in front of the same dashpot. So a Burgers material held at a
fixed strain gives up stress sooner than a Maxwell material at first and
later than it afterwards. Held at a fixed stress it is ahead throughout, by
the Kelvin element's strain. A fault that slips and stays slipped loads the
rock around it somewhere between the two, and which it is nearer is a matter
for the solve.

For the model on this site, $k=1$ and $\tau_K=0.1\,\tau_M$: the two times
are $0.049$ and $2.05$ Maxwell times, and the two fractions $0.525$ and
$0.475$.

**What it asks of the method.** Of the solver, nothing. A state is an
elastic solve at $\tilde\mu(s)$, and only the function is different. At
$s=\infty$ the modulus is $\mu$ and at $s\to0$ it is zero, as for a Maxwell
material, so the earthquake and the fully relaxed state are the Maxwell
model's own. A Burgers substrate changes the path between two ends that it
leaves alone. Poisson's ratio has two poles more, from
$3K+4\tilde\mu(s)=0$.

Of the window it asks one thing. A fit is trusted only inside the window its
points were laid out for, and the transient is over in a few $\tau_K$. So
the window is carried below it, from $0.02$ instead of $0.1$, which is three
more sample points: sixteen, and the elastic state makes seventeen.

The fit is *not* told the material's two times. Its own relaxation times
are fixed at three per decade whatever relaxes, and a body's response is a
sum over many more exponentials than its material has. On transforms with
two poles the fit holds as it does with one (the table under [Back to
time](#back-to-time)).

**What it does to a megathrust.** The megathrust of this site again, on its
finer mesh, over a Burgers substrate and over a Maxwell one, each solved at
the same seventeen states. At the earthquake the two are one solve. After
it, the displacement since the earthquake at mid-strike, Burgers over
Maxwell (rms over the profile, both components):

| $t/\tau_M$ | 0.03 | 0.1 | 0.3 | 1 | 3 | 10 | 30 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Burgers over Maxwell | 9.2 | 6.5 | 3.4 | 1.58 | 1.09 | 0.985 | 0.996 | 1.000 |

At a tenth of a Maxwell time the ground over the Burgers substrate has moved
six and a half times as far, as far as the Maxwell model has by $0.7$. The
lead is gone at $6\,\tau_M$. From there the Burgers model is the one behind,
by 1.6&nbsp;% at most, near $11\,\tau_M$, and by a hundred the two are the
same. So a megathrust loads its substrate much more nearly as a held stress
than as a held strain: the transient shows almost wholly as a head start.
A fault locked between earthquakes every $20\,\tau_M$ moves differently by
27&nbsp;% a twentieth of the way through its cycle and by 1&nbsp;% from the
middle of it on.

Against the layered reference with the same Burgers substrate, the model's
postseismic displacement is within 2.5 to 2.8&nbsp;% from $0.03$ to
$10\,\tau_M$, the transient included, and within 4.3&nbsp;% at a hundred.
Its Maxwell twin on the same mesh gives 2.5 to 2.8&nbsp;% and 4.3&nbsp;%: the
transient costs the method nothing. The ratios in the table are the model's
and the reference's alike, to 0.2&nbsp;%.

## Earthquakes that repeat

Let the same step of slip recur every $T$, for ever. At a time $t$ after the
latest one, the relaxation of all of them is a sum over the past, and for
each exponential of the fit that sum is geometric:

$$
\sum_{n\ge0}e^{-(t+nT)/\tau_j}=\frac{e^{-t/\tau_j}}{1-e^{-T/\tau_j}} .
$$

So a cycle costs nothing beyond the fit: it is another set of weights. The
construction is that of Savage and Prescott (1978) for a strike-slip fault
and of Thatcher and Rundle (1984) for a subduction zone, with the sum done
in closed form.

What geodesy measures between earthquakes is the velocity of the ground
while the fault is locked. A fault locked between earthquakes of slip $b$ is
the same fault slipping steadily at $V=b/T$, less the difference, and the
difference is periodic. With $u(t)$ the response to a unit step, the
velocity measured from steady slip, per unit $V$, is

$$
v(t)=T\sum_{n\ge0}\dot u(t+nT)\;-\;u(\infty),\qquad 0\lt t\lt T.
$$

Three things follow.

**Its mean is elastic back slip.** Integrating the sum over one cycle
integrates $\dot u$ over all time, which is $u(\infty)-u(0^+)$, so

$$
\frac1T\int_0^T v\,dt=-\,u(0^+).
$$

The back-slip model (Savage 1983) is the average of the cycle whatever
relaxes. Relaxation only moves velocity between early and late.

**It asks less of the window than a relaxed state does.** What the response
does long after a period enters the sum and the limit alike and all but
cancels. The window must hold a few periods, not a settled body.

**It has a reference that uses no fit.** The train of earthquakes is
periodic, so its response is a Fourier series, and the coefficient at
$s_n=2\pi i\,n/T$ is the elastic solution at the moduli of $s_n$:

$$
v(t)=-u(0^+)+2\,\operatorname{Re}\sum_{n\ge1}\big[\tilde u(s_n)-u(0^+)\big]\,e^{s_nt}.
$$

On a layered reference, where a solution at imaginary $s$ is cheap, the
fitted velocity agrees with that series to $4\times10^{-4}$ of its peak at
$T=20\,\tau$.

## Gravity

In this section $w=\rho\,\mathrm g$ is the weight of a unit volume, so that
$g$ keeps its meaning.

Before it is deformed the body is at rest under its own weight, with a
hydrostatic prestress: $\boldsymbol\sigma_0=-p_0\,\mathbf I$,
$\nabla p_0=-w\,\hat{\mathbf z}$, with $\hat{\mathbf z}$ up. A displacement carries prestressed
material with it, so there are two increments of stress to tell apart: the
change in a particle, $\boldsymbol\tau^L$, and the change at a fixed point
of space,

$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\boldsymbol\tau^L-w\,(\mathbf u\cdot\hat{\mathbf z})\,\mathbf I .
$$

A column lifted by $u_z$ puts at each point the material from $u_z$ deeper:
more pressure at the point, none in any particle. With a uniform density in
each region and no self-gravitation, equilibrium of the perturbation is

$$
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau+w\,(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)\,\hat{\mathbf z}=\mathbf 0,
$$

the second term being the weight of material whose density the deformation
has changed.

Two things are then done, and both are exact for an incompressible
material. The internal buoyancy $w\,(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)\,\hat{\mathbf z}$
is dropped, and Hooke's law is applied to $\boldsymbol\tau$. What is left
inside every region is

$$
\nabla\cdot\big(\mathbf C:\boldsymbol\epsilon(\mathbf u)\big)=\mathbf 0,
$$

the equation the kernels already solve. Gravity has left the volume. For a
compressible material the error is of relative order $wL/K$, with $L$ the
scale of the deformation: 6&nbsp;% per 100&nbsp;km at $K=50$&nbsp;GPa.

Where it went is the boundaries. What is continuous across a material
surface is the traction of $\boldsymbol\tau^L$, so in terms of
$\mathbf t=\boldsymbol\tau\,\mathbf n$:

| surface | condition |
|---|---|
| the free surface, under an applied load $\bar{\mathbf t}$ | $\mathbf t=\bar{\mathbf t}-w\,(\mathbf u\cdot\hat{\mathbf z})\,\mathbf n$ |
| an interface between regions 1 and 2 | $\mathbf t_2=\mathbf t_1-(w_2-w_1)\,(\mathbf u\cdot\hat{\mathbf z})\,\mathbf n$ |
| a fault, inside one region | $\mathbf t$ continuous: no term |
| the sides and base of the box | no term |

A fault has no term because $\boldsymbol\tau$ is the stress at a point, and
the two faces of a fault at one point of space see the same one. A wall has
none because it is a cut and not a cliff: the rock beyond it, at the same
density, loads it with exactly the term that would otherwise appear.

So gravity is a traction proportional to the displacement, on the free
surface and on every interface across which the density changes. In the
equations it is one more block on columns that are already there, the
kernel $\mathbf G^\varepsilon$ applied to $w$ times geometry times the
displacement. The weight is not a modulus, so correspondence stands: a state
under gravity is an elastic solve.

It contains the classical results. A load of wavenumber $k$ on a half-space
meets the stiffness

$$
w+\tilde\mu(s)\,g\,k ,
$$

whose two terms balance at the buoyancy wavenumber of Barbot and Fialko
(2010). For an incompressible Maxwell half-space its one pole is at
$\tau+2\eta k/w$: the relaxation time of a viscous half-space under gravity
(Haskell 1935), plus the Maxwell time. And a substrate that has relaxed is
hydrostatic, so the plate on it feels $w_p$ per unit deflection at its top
and $w_s-w_p$ at its base, $w_s$ in all, which is flexural isostasy.

One restriction comes with it. Where the heavier rock is on top, a body
that relaxes has a mode that grows, and a fit made of decaying exponentials
has nothing to answer it with. Such a model is refused.

## The walls

A boundary method built on full-space kernels has no half-space in it. The
model is a box, and what its walls do is part of the answer. For a fault
that cuts the plate it is most of the late answer: once the substrate lets
go, the two sides of a megathrust converge as rigid blocks, and it is the
walls that stop them.

| megathrust 800&nbsp;km long, walls 800&nbsp;km away | fixed | free | window |
|---|---|---|---|
| horizontal, postseismic, at $\tau$ / $10\tau$ / $100\tau$ | 0.065 / 0.102 / 0.140 | 0.065 / 0.088 / 0.154 | 0.021 / 0.017 / 0.023 |
| convergence of the two sides, over the reference's | 0.94 / 0.90 / 0.81 | 1.04 / 1.07 / 1.11 | 0.98 / 0.99 / 0.99 |

Fixed walls hold the plate and free ones let it go too far; the two bracket
the truth and neither is what the plate beyond a wall does. A spring on the
wall does not mend it, because that plate is stiff early and slack late.

**The window.** Each wall is given, as its displacement, the displacement
that the model's own prescribed slip produces in an unbounded medium that
has a solution of its own: a plate over a half-space. The box then solves
only what that reference does not know, which is its interior and whatever
is in it, and the reference carries the far field. The datum depends on the
moduli, so it is evaluated at every state. It is a right-hand side; the
operator does not change.

**The reference** is a rectangular fault in a plate over a half-space, in
three dimensions, under the same buoyancy. Transform horizontally. In the
frame of a wavevector, with $\xi$ along it and $t$ across it, elasticity
splits into a P-SV system for $(u_\xi,u_z)$ and an SH system for $u_t$,
which buoyancy does not enter. Slip is an eigenstrain
$\mathbf E=\operatorname{sym}(\mathbf b\otimes\mathbf n)$, and at its depth
it is a jump in the fields that are otherwise continuous:

$$
[u_\xi]=2E_{\xi z},\qquad
[u_z]=E_{zz}+\frac{\lambda\,(E_{\xi\xi}+E_{tt})}{\lambda+2\mu},\qquad
[\tau_{\xi z}]=ik\,\frac{4\mu(\lambda+\mu)\,E_{\xi\xi}+2\mu\lambda\,E_{tt}}{\lambda+2\mu},
$$

$$
[u_t]=2E_{tz},\qquad[\tau_{tz}]=2ik\mu\,E_{\xi t}.
$$

The fault is integrated along strike in closed form and down dip by
quadrature, and cut where it crosses the interface, each part in the moduli
of its own layer. At the surface the result is Okada's (1985) closed form
for the half-space plus the transform of the smooth difference from it; at
depth, where a wall needs it, the spectrum is tapered. It shares nothing
with the boundary elements, which is what lets it also be what they are
compared with.

**What a window does not see.** It is exact for what its reference solves
and blind to everything else: a slip that is solved and not prescribed, a
slab. That part of the far field falls off with distance, so for it alone
the size of the box is a parameter again.

## Two meshes

The elements are constant on each triangle. For strike slip that is enough.
Dip slip over a relaxed substrate bends the plate, and the bending comes out
short by an amount that is first order in three lengths at once: the
elements of the plate's top, those of its base, and the mollification width
of the base. Each is modest and all have one sign. On a 30&nbsp;km plate
with elements of 10&nbsp;km on its top and 15&nbsp;km on its base, and a
width of 1.5&nbsp;km, the trough behind a thrust is 12&nbsp;% shallow once
the plate is afloat, and 6&nbsp;% with all three halved.

An error that is first order in $h$ is $u_h=u+c\,h$ to leading order. Two
meshes with the three lengths in one ratio $r$, coarse over fine, give two
equations for $u$ and $c$, and

$$
u_{\text{limit}}=\frac{r\,u_{\text{fine}}-u_{\text{coarse}}}{r-1}.
$$

For the megathrust on this site, in a window 400&nbsp;km to its walls,
against the reference in three dimensions:

| misfit | top 10, base 15, width 1.5&nbsp;km | half of each | the limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| at the earthquake, horizontal and vertical | 0.007, 0.019 | 0.004, 0.009 | 0.001, 0.004 |
| postseismic, at $\tau$ / $10\tau$ / $100\tau$ | 0.055 / 0.051 / 0.086 | 0.026 / 0.026 / 0.043 | 0.007 / 0.010 / 0.016 |
| its vertical part | 0.199 / 0.163 / 0.172 | 0.095 / 0.083 / 0.085 | 0.026 / 0.031 / 0.032 |
| velocity of the locked fault, $T=20\tau$, early / late | 0.036 / 0.018 | 0.017 / 0.010 | 0.005 / 0.004 |

Every row halves with the mesh, which is what first order means, and the
limit is what that leaves. It removes this error and no other, and it has a
price: whatever the two meshes do *not* share is magnified by
$(r+1)/(r-1)$, three for a ratio of two and five for 1.5. Where the second
mesh cannot be made much finer than the first, the limit is not taken.

## A fault that creeps

So far slip was given. Let a fault instead be solved, and let it resist the
*rate* at which it slips: one side drags the other with a shear traction
$\eta_f\,\dot{\mathbf b}$. If nothing has slipped before the step, the
transform of that is $\eta_f\,s\,\hat{\mathbf b}$, and for step data the
state at $s$ has on that fault the condition

$$
\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma-\kappa(s)\,\mathbf b=\Delta\mathbf t,
\qquad\kappa(s)=\eta_f\,s .
$$

The elastic traction of every source, the fault's own slip included, less a
stiffness times that slip. That is an elastic problem like every other
state, with the slip among its unknowns, so afterslip needs no time-stepper
either. At $s=\infty$ the stiffness is infinite and the fault is locked: it
does not slip at the instant of the earthquake. As $s\to0$ the stiffness
vanishes and the fault ends where it carries no traction. In between, its
slip is whatever the rest of the model loads it with.

The law is stated as the shear zone it stands for, of width $h$ and of a
material with Maxwell time $\tau_c$ at the modulus of the rock around it,
so that $\eta_f=\mu\,\tau_c/h$: again a time and a ratio. A patch of size
$L$ unloads in about $\tau_c\,L/h$. Friction that strengthens with rate is
the same law. Linearised about a steady rate $V$,
$\tau=\sigma_n\big[\mu_0+(a-b)\ln(V'/V)\big]$ has the slope
$(a-b)\,\sigma_n/V$, which is $\eta_f$ (Marone and others 1991; Perfettini
and Avouac 2004).

It has an exact anchor. A penny-shaped crack whose faces drag with
$\kappa_0/\sqrt{1-r^2/a^2}$ keeps the elliptical slip of the crack without
drag, because that slip's traction is uniform, and its peak is
$\Delta\tau/(k_E+\kappa_0)$ with $k_E$ the crack's own stiffness. In an
elastic body that is one exponential with the time $\eta_0/k_E$. In a
Maxwell body the crack's stiffness relaxes too, and the fault's creep and
the body's are one transform. The solved moment is within 0.7&nbsp;% of it at
every $s$ and at every time, which is the crack's own error in the elastic
problem.

## Stress, and the interior

No unknown lives inside a region, but the field does. Once the boundaries
are solved, the formula under [One elastic problem per
state](#one-elastic-problem-per-state) gives the displacement at any
interior point, and its gradient gives the strain. Both hold at every state,
so the weights of a time apply to them as they do to the boundary:

$$
\boldsymbol\sigma(\mathbf x,t)=\mathbf w(t)\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol\sigma}(\mathbf x),
\qquad
\tilde{\boldsymbol\sigma}_k=\mathbf C(s_k):\big(\nabla\tilde{\mathbf u}_k-\boldsymbol\epsilon^*\big).
$$

Each state's stress is formed with that state's moduli and has the
eigenstress removed at those moduli. In time that makes the eigenstress
hereditary: after a step of slip it decays with the shear modulus of the
rock it is in, as it should, since it is a stress the material would have
to hold.

A quantity that is not linear in the stress is formed last. The deviatoric
stress drawn on the sections is $\sqrt{3J_2}$ of the tensor at each time,
never a combination of its values at the states.

### Close to a boundary

The formula is exact for the displacement and the traction on the boundary.
What a solve returns is one value of each to an element, and close to a face
the difference shows. The displacement enters through
$\mathbf T^\varepsilon$, a double layer, and a double layer whose density
steps from one element to the next has a dislocation line along the edge
between them, as strong as the step. From further than an element away the
lines cancel. From closer they do not. On a box under uniform strain, with
the exact value on every element and nothing solved, the stress is wrong by

| depth below the face, in elements | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.5 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| one value to an element | 19&nbsp;% | 10&nbsp;% | 3&nbsp;% | 0.5&nbsp;% |
| a continuous displacement | 0.1&nbsp;% | &lt;&nbsp;0.1&nbsp;% | &lt;&nbsp;0.1&nbsp;% | &lt;&nbsp;0.1&nbsp;% |

No way of solving for one value to an element changes the first row. So the
sections are read differently near a face, and the solve is not touched.

- **The displacement is made continuous.** At every vertex and every edge
  midpoint of a boundary's mesh it takes the value of the quadratic that best
  fits the elements two rings around: a piecewise-quadratic density with no
  steps. A fault's trace divides the fit, because across a fault the
  displacement does jump.
- **The traction is left as it was solved.** Its steps matter little, and
  where a fault cuts an interface it has a real concentration that a
  smoothing would spread: on the megathrust that moved the whole interior by
  7 to 9&nbsp;%.
- **The width is narrowed for the reading.** Within about two widths
  $\varepsilon$ of a face the mollified field falls to half its value there,
  and beyond that layer the blob's tail still costs the square of the width
  over the distance. With no steps left to expose, the width of those
  boundaries is taken a fiftieth as large, and both all but go. With one
  value to an element the same narrowing makes things worse.
- **A point on a boundary is read from just inside it.** Exactly on a face
  the formula returns half the value, and a hair off it what is left of the
  elements shows as isolated bright points. A point within a tenth of an
  element of a boundary is read from a tenth of an element inside, and a
  point exactly on an interface is counted with the first of its two regions,
  by rule: its solid angle cannot say.

Only the elements within two of their own lengths of a point are read this
way, and the rest as before, so a section costs an eighth more time. On the
megathrust the scatter from cell to cell of the mean stress, within a quarter
of an element of the surface or of the plate's base, falls from 4&nbsp;% to
0.1&nbsp;% at the median and from over 100&nbsp;% to under 2&nbsp;% for the
worst twentieth of the cells. More than two elements from every boundary
nothing moves. Where a fault meets a boundary a concentration of stress
remains, and it is the model's own.

## What it is held to

Each piece above is tested against something that shares nothing with it.

| case | what it exercises | agreement |
|---|---|---|
| a dislocation in a relaxing full space | a fault inside the region that relaxes | kernels to $10^{-11}$ at every $\nu(s)$ |
| a sphere in a relaxing host (Eshelby) | two regions, $\lambda$ live, two relaxation times | 1.6&nbsp;%, flat in $s$ |
| torsion of a two-layer cylinder | an interface, a free wall, a fixed base | 5&nbsp;% on 847 elements, flat in $s$ |
| a penny crack under a sustained stress drop | a solved fault that creeps for ever | 0.5&nbsp;% in moment |
| a strike-slip fault in a plate over a Burgers substrate | the transient, and a window that reaches below it | within 3&nbsp;% in the largest box, inside the transient and after it |
| layered shells on a rigid core, radial buoyancy | gravity, per $s$ and in time | within 2.6&nbsp;% |
| the layered reference against half-space closed forms | the reference itself | $7\times10^{-5}$ on the walls of a box |
| a megathrust through the plate, window and two meshes | everything at once | 1.6&nbsp;% in time, 0.5&nbsp;% through the cycle |
| a box under uniform strain, read a tenth of an element below its free face | the reading close to a boundary | 1.2&nbsp;% at the worst point, where the elements' values give 19&nbsp;% |

In every solved case the answer in time carries the elastic problem's own
spatial error and nothing more. The closed forms are inverted on a contour,
which shares nothing with the real-axis fit.

## Beyond linearity

Everything above rests on the problem being linear and on the kind of each
boundary condition staying fixed. A mantle that flows by a power law,
friction that is not linearised, a fault that chooses when to slip: none of
these has a transform that helps.

For the boundary conditions there is a way through that keeps the volume
empty, and it is a design, not something built. Multiplying by
$s/(s+\beta)$ is, in time, a memory variable
$m(t)=\int_0^te^{-\beta(t-t')}\,\dot x(t')\,dt'$, which obeys
$\dot m=-\beta\,m+\dot x$ and updates in one line a step:

$$
m^n=e^{-\beta\Delta t}\,m^{n-1}+\psi\,(x^n-x^{n-1}),\qquad
\psi=\frac{1-e^{-\beta\Delta t}}{\beta\,\Delta t}.
$$

Because every operator is geometry times a few such functions, the history
of a region is a few vectors the size of its boundary unknowns. An implicit
step is an elastic solve at effective moduli that lie between the unrelaxed
and the relaxed ones, and one factorisation serves every step of the same
length. A nonlinear *rheology* is another matter: its viscous strain is a
field in the volume, and a method that has no volume has nowhere to keep it.
