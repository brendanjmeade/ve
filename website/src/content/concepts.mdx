## A relaxing Earth is an elastic one, many times

An earthquake is fast and the Earth's response to it is slow. The elastic
plate that broke rests on rock that flows, and for decades afterwards the
stress the earthquake put into that rock relaxes, loading the plate again as
it goes. The standard ways to compute this put a mesh through the volume, or
assume flat layers.

Neither is needed if the rheology is linear. Take the Laplace transform in
time, $\hat f(s)=\int_0^\infty f(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt$. A linear viscoelastic
material becomes an elastic one whose shear modulus depends on $s$; for a
Maxwell material with relaxation time $\tau=\eta/\mu$,

$$
\tilde\mu(s)=\mu\,\frac{s}{s+1/\tau}.
$$

The bulk modulus stays elastic: rock flows in shear and does not flow in
compression. If everything that is prescribed is switched on as a step, then
$\tilde u(s)=s\,\hat u(s)$ is exactly the *elastic* solution of the same
problem with $\tilde\mu(s)$ in place of $\mu$. Large $s$ is the instant of
the earthquake, and small $s$ is long after:

$$
u(0^+)=\tilde u(\infty),\qquad u(\infty)=\tilde u(0).
$$

So nothing new has to be computed. The boundary element method that solves
the elastic problem solves each of these, on the same boundary mesh, with
only the moduli of the relaxing region changed. The substrate goes from an
elastic solid to something close to a fluid as $s$ falls, and the method has
to stay accurate all the way there, which costs one rule: a region that
relaxes carries one mollification width on its whole closed boundary.

## From fourteen solves to every time

The models here are solved at real $s$, log-spaced at three points per
decade across the times wanted and half a decade beyond each end. For times
from 0.1 to 100 relaxation times that is thirteen values, and the elastic
state makes fourteen.

A relaxing body's response to a step is a constant and decaying exponentials,
so the solutions are fitted with relaxation times fixed at the reciprocals of
the sample points:

$$
\tilde u(s)=a_0+\sum_j a_j\,\frac{s}{s+1/\tau_j}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
u(t)=a_0+\sum_j a_j\,e^{-t/\tau_j}.
$$

The fit is linear and involves no solution values, so it is one small matrix,
the same for every unknown and every point: a vector of fourteen weights for
each time. A displacement at time $t$ is those weights times the fourteen
solutions. The velocity is a second set of weights, from the derivative of
the same exponentials.

Two things follow that matter for reading this site.

**The fit is trusted inside its window and nowhere else.** Nothing constrains
it before the shortest relaxation time or after the longest. The clock on the
[Examples](../examples/) page stops at 100 because the solves stop there.

**The weights are large and of both signs.** They sum to about 200 in
magnitude, 800 for a velocity, and cancel. A velocity at three points per
decade carries about 1&nbsp;%. It is also why the page is not handed the
fourteen solutions: rounding each one would be multiplied by the weights. It
is handed a few orthonormal combinations of them, with the weights already
applied in double precision.

## The cycle of a locked fault

Earthquakes on a fault repeat. If the same step recurs every $T$ for ever,
the relaxation of all the past ones is a geometric series in each
exponential, which costs nothing. The quantity geodesy measures between
earthquakes is the velocity of the ground while the fault is locked. Measured
from the same fault slipping steadily, and per unit slip rate, it is

$$
v(t)=T\sum_{n\ge0}\dot u(t+nT)\;-\;u(\infty),
$$

at time $t$ after the latest earthquake. Its mean over a cycle is minus the
elastic response, whatever relaxes: the back-slip model of Savage (1983) is
the average of the cycle, and relaxation only moves velocity between early
and late in it. That is what the *locked fault* views show, for three
recurrence intervals.

## A box whose walls are a window

There is no half-space in a boundary element method built on full-space
kernels. The model is a box, and what its walls do is part of the model.

For most problems a large box is enough. A fault that cuts the plate is not
one of them. Once the substrate lets go, the two sides of a megathrust
converge as rigid blocks, and it is the walls that stop them. Walls held
fixed 800&nbsp;km away allow 0.81 of the convergence the unbounded problem
has; from 400&nbsp;km, 0.61. Free walls let the plates go too far, 1.11.

So the walls are made a *window*. Each wall carries the displacement that
the model's own slip produces in an unbounded medium, a plate over a
half-space, evaluated at every Laplace state. That reference is a wavenumber
integral for a finite rectangular fault in three dimensions; it shares
nothing with the boundary elements. With it, the horizontal motion of the
megathrust is within 2 to 3&nbsp;% from a box only 400&nbsp;km to its walls,
and the answer no longer depends on the box.

A window is exact for what its reference solves and blind to everything
else. The reference knows the prescribed slip in a layered half-space. It
does not know a fault whose slip is solved, or a slab.

## A plate that bends

The elements are piecewise constant. For strike slip that is enough. Dip
slip over a relaxed substrate bends the plate, and the vertical motion comes
out short by an amount first order in the element size on the plate's two
faces: 12&nbsp;% with 10&nbsp;km elements on a 30&nbsp;km plate, 6&nbsp;% at
half that.

A first-order error can be removed with two meshes. With the element sizes in
a ratio $r$, coarse over fine,

$$
u_{\text{limit}}=\frac{r\,u_{\text{fine}}-u_{\text{coarse}}}{r-1}.
$$

This is the *two-mesh limit* in the megathrust's profile. The surface drawn
in three dimensions is the finer mesh as it was solved, about 9&nbsp;% short
in the vertical after the earthquake; the limit is within 3&nbsp;%. Window
and limit together put the megathrust within 1.6&nbsp;% of the reference at
1, 10 and 100 relaxation times, and within 0.5&nbsp;% through its cycle.

## Gravity, on the boundary

Gravity resists vertical motion once the substrate has relaxed: lifting the
surface makes a load, and so does moving the base of the plate against the
denser rock below. Both are tractions on boundaries. A face with normal
$\mathbf n$ that has moved by $\mathbf u$ carries an extra traction

$$
\Delta\mathbf t=-\Delta\rho\,g\,(\mathbf u\cdot\hat{\mathbf z})\,\mathbf n,
$$

with $\Delta\rho$ the density of the rock on the free surface, and the
contrast across it on an interface. Nothing enters the volume. It is exact
for an incompressible material and off by about $\rho g L/K$ otherwise.

## A fault that creeps

A fault can be made to resist its slip *rate*: a thin shear zone with
viscosity, dragging with a traction $\eta_f\,\dot{\mathbf b}$. In the Laplace
domain that is a fault with a stiffness,

$$
\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma-\kappa(s)\,\mathbf b=\Delta\mathbf t,
\qquad\kappa(s)=\eta_f\,s,
$$

so its slip is solved at each state like any other unknown, and afterslip
needs no time-stepper either. At the instant of the earthquake the stiffness
is infinite and the fault is locked; long afterwards it carries no traction.
Rate-strengthening friction linearised about a steady rate is the same law.

In the afterslip model the megathrust slips in the plate and its lower
reach, from 30 to 45&nbsp;km, creeps. The afterslip averages 0.22 of the
slip and is complete by about ten relaxation times. It is an early signal:
at one relaxation time the surface differs from the model without it by a
third, and by a hundred the two cannot be told apart. A solved fault runs on
the dense solver only, so that model's mesh is coarse and its vertical motion
carries 14 to 23&nbsp;%.

## A slab

A layered half-space cannot hold a slab. Boundaries can: the footwall plate
is continued down dip as an elastic slab, and the interface between plate and
mantle wraps it. With a slab to 150&nbsp;km the uplift behind the trench
barely relaxes, and the postseismic displacement differs from the model
without one by 61, 70 and 43&nbsp;% at 1, 10 and 100 relaxation times.

Two limits are stated on that model. The slab is welded to the mantle below
the megathrust, and plate and mantle share one density, because a lighter
slab hanging in a heavier mantle is unstable once anything relaxes. And the
walls do not know about the slab: between a 500 and an 800&nbsp;km box the
horizontal agrees to 2&nbsp;%, but the vertical at 100 relaxation times
differs by 31&nbsp;%.

## What is not here

Everything on this site is linear. A power-law mantle, rate-and-state
friction and a fault that chooses when to slip all need a time-stepper, which
is designed and not built. Slip is uniform on each rupture. The substrate is
a Maxwell material with one relaxation time; a Burgers material is
implemented and not shown.
